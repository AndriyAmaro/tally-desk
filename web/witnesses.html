Witnesses: clocks nobody here controls

A hash chain proves a record was not edited after it was written, but whoever runs it could rewrite the whole chain, or cut the end off. What stops that is a copy of the head held somewhere the operator cannot reach. Every cycle the head is stamped in Bitcoin (OpenTimestamps); the heads up to dossier 791 were also signed, with no key of ours, in the public Sigstore transparency log by the earlier project's workflow; and it all sits in git history. This page re-checks each of those in your browser: it reads the proof, asks two independent block explorers and the transparency log, and compares what they say with the ledger it just downloaded. Nothing below is a number we typed.

1. Does the ledger still agree with every head that was stamped?

checking…

Each hour the head (sequence number and hash) is saved as ledger/timestamps/<seq>.json and stamped. If someone cut the end off the ledger, or rewrote its history and re-hashed it so the chain still verifies, the ledger would disagree with a head that was already stamped. This is the check that catches both.

2. Bitcoin, through OpenTimestamps

The proof says: this digest, put through these hash steps, becomes this value, which is written in the header of Bitcoin block N. Your browser reads the proof (ots.js), recomputes the digest of the saved head, replays the steps, and asks two public explorers for that block's merkle root. A green line means the head existed, byte for byte, no later than that block was mined, and neither explorer nor we can change that.

HeadStamped inProof matches the headLedger agreesBlock checked by

The newest proofs are pending for a few hours: the calendars have promised to include the head, and the Bitcoin block comes later. That is normal, and the table says so instead of showing a green line it has not earned.

3. Sigstore transparency log

asking the public log…

For the heads up to dossier 791 a workflow of the earlier project (.github/workflows/rekor.yml in AndriyAmaro/verdict-desk, not in this repository) signed the saved head with no key and no secret: GitHub tells Sigstore which repository and which workflow is running, Sigstore issues a certificate that lasts minutes and names that identity, and the signature goes into Rekor, a public log that records its own time. Your browser asks Rekor for each entry, checks that the digest the log holds is the sha256 of the head it just downloaded, and reads the certificate to see who signed. It does not check the certificate chain or the log's inclusion proof: for that, run cosign verify-blob --bundle ledger/rekor/<seq>.bundle.json --certificate-identity-regexp '^https://github.com/AndriyAmaro/verdict-desk/.github/workflows/rekor.yml@refs/' --certificate-oidc-issuer https://token.actions.githubusercontent.com ledger/timestamps/<seq>.json. The log's clock and the signer's identity are the point: neither belongs to the person who runs the ledger.

4. Git history

asking GitHub…

The weakest witness: the commit times belong to the operator's own account. Its value is that every clone made earlier still holds the old history, so a rewrite would show.

5. We attacked our own verifier

loading…

Reproduce it: python3 -m verdict_desk battery (the seed is the head hash, so you get the same attacks). The two attacks the hash chain cannot see alone are listed on purpose: cutting the end off, and rewriting history and re-hashing every later record. They pass verify by design and are caught only by the stamped heads in section 1.

What none of this proves

  • That a decision was right, or that a source said something true. A timestamp proves the bytes existed by a time, not that they are correct.
  • Who wrote the record. Integrity after recording is the claim, not authorship at collection time.
  • That nobody could attack the way we did not think of. The battery counts the attacks we tried, no more.

Tally Desk on Otari decides on paper trades only. No real-money order is ever placed.
